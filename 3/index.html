    BOB DEMO-BUILDER MODE

    BUSINESS PARTNERS | DEPLOYING WITH DEMO BUILDER

    AUDIENCE: BUSINESS PARTNERS

    This chapter is the Business Partner path to a working Demo Builder deployment, and it is fully self-contained: the internal CE Bob Marketplace, GitHub PAT, and MCP credential steps documented in Chapters 1 and 2 are not required here. Instead, the Demo Builder arrives as a packaged download from IBM Seismic, with everything pre-loaded and ready to use. IBMers should follow Chapter 1 | Setup and registration and Chapter 2 | Deploying with Demo Builder instead — both paths arrive at the same working demonstration.

    i. The Business Partner path

    The previous chapter carried an IBMer from a one-sentence use case to a running, client-ready demonstration. This chapter delivers the same outcome for Business Partners via a slightly different, but relatively simplified path. Rather than assembling the environment piece by piece (Marketplace extension, access token, MCP credentials), business partners will download a single packaged folder, open it within the Bob IDE, and find the IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode already loaded and waiting. From there, the workflow mirrors what an IBM seller experiences: a guided intake, an autonomous build, a local launch, a review-and-refine pass, and a generated demo script.

    PRE-REQUISITES

    • IBM Bob is installed and open
    • You can access the IBM Seismic download link provided in the steps below

    Because this flow builds in mock mode— no functional credentials are needed and the environment is fully offline (running locally only) —nothing beyond the package itself is required.

    A WORD ON WORKING WITH GENERATIVE AI

    IBM Bob is powered by generative AI and LLMs, and a defining trait of these systems is that they are non-deterministic — unlike the deterministic tools most developers are accustomed to. In practice, that means the same prompt can produce different code from one run to the next. This is both a strength and a quirk of the technology, and it is something to work with rather than against.

    Precision is what tips the odds in your favor. The more clearly you describe what you want, the more closely Bob's output will mirror your intent; conversely, the smallest change in wording can meaningfully change the generated code. Throughout this lab, your results may differ from the solutions shown — and that is expected, not a defect. Whenever an output diverges, you have three good options: refine the prompt and try again, edit the generated code by hand, or simply proceed if the variation still compiles and runs. Human review remains essential at every step, and never more so than while you are still learning the tool.

    ii. Why this matters

    Take note of the division of labor as you work through this chapter, because it is the very discipline this course has been reinforcing from the beginning: Bob does the building; the human-in-the-loop (you) makes the final decisions and calls the shots. The intake questions that the Demo Builder poses at the start— the build approach, environment, deployment target, scenario, and so on —are critical decision points that you must answer. Once those decisions have been recorded and sanctioned, Bob executes those instructions autonomously: generating, verifying, testing, and (as you will witness first-hand) catching and repairing its own bugs. Throughout this process, you maintain your role as the principle arbiter for approving additional actions, validating the results, and advancing (or re-orienting) the workflow.

    There is a second lesson embedded here as well. Despite "mock mode" having no live models or real environments backing it, the finalized demonstration is a complete, navigable, Carbon-styled application with preset AI scenarios, a results view, an architecture view, and a generated walkthrough script. For engagements where you are short on time (or short on Bob tokens before the month rolls over), these types of lightweight demonstrations can be immensely valuable in a pinch.

    Finally, there is a practical dimension worth internalizing. The full build consumes roughly 20 minutes of time and approximately 15–20 Bobcoins. Even in mock mode, with no live model behind it, the result is a complete, navigable, Carbon-styled application with preset AI scenarios, auto-generated work orders, an architecture view, and a client-ready walkthrough script. For a Business Partner, that means a credible IBM demonstration no longer requires provisioned environments, credentials, or weeks of scarce technical talent as its price of admission.

    iii. Acquire the Demo Builder

    1. Download the IBM-Pre-Sales-Demo-Builder.zip file from the following location:

      https://ibm.seismic.com/Link/Content/DCMhGH8HMFdJ2G4QRdFjDJT6m438

    1. Once downloaded, locate the file and double-click to begin extraction.

    1. Extract the IBM-Pre-Sales-Demo-Builder .zip file to a directory (of your choosing) on your local machine.

    1. Return to the IBM Bob IDE.

      If it is not already open, click Explorer (Ctrl+Shift+E) to open the file explorer panel within the IDE.

    1. Click Open Folder.

    1. Locate the extracted version (Step 3) of the Pre-Sales Demo Builder and select it. (For Windows, this most likely landed within your Downloads folder.)

      Click "Select folder" to confirm the choice.

      SELECT THE EXTRACTED VERSION

      Ensure you select the unzipped version — the resulting folder produced after you pressed Extract all!

    1. With the unzipped folder now open within the IBM Bob IDE, click the mode selector button to expand the window. You will find IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder automatically loaded and ready to use.

      Select IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode.

    iv. Brief the Demo Builder

    1. With IBM Bob in IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode, paste the following prompt in the Agentic Sidebar chat window:

      I need a demo of an AI agent that monitors IoT sensor data from factory equipment, predicts failures before they happen, and automatically creates maintenance work orders with recommended parts and technicians.

    1. IBM Bob's Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode asks its first question: how should the AI agent for this app be built?

      • In future builds, you may select whichever option best fits the product you are demoing
      • For this example, and for ease of use, choose watsonx.ai
      • Click "Use suggestion: watsonx.ai App demo (RECOMMENDED) — LangGraph ReAct agent in FastAPI, Carbon dashboard with real-time sensor charts, anomaly det..."

      YOUR OPTIONS WILL LIKELY DIFFER

      The exact options presented will very likely be different in your run — the key is simply to select the option carrying watsonx.ai. The watsonx Orchestrate demo setup is more advanced; additional details can be found here: https://github.ibm.com/ClientEngineering/bob/blob/main/Pre-Sales-Demo-Builder/Sample-Reciepies/07-Predictive-Maintenance-Agent.md

    1. Next, you may be asked what audience the demonstration should be presented to.

      The selection does not particularly matter for this exercise — here, we chose: "C-suite / VP of Operations / Plant Director — 15-minute executive overview [...]"

    1. Next, you are asked whether you actually have a live watsonx.ai environment.

      This selection is crucial

      Select "Mock mode only [...]".

      In real engagements, you can incorporate live watsonx.ai API keys (or other IBM products) to add genuine functionality to your demo via IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode. For the example presented within this guide, however, you will work in mock mode. As you will see, realistic and impressive results are achievable even upon purely synthetic data.

    1. Similarly, the next selection is also crucial. You are asked how you wish to deploy the app — for now, you must select an option that allows the demo to run locally.

      In this case, select "Use suggestion: Run locally first (RECOMMENDED default) [...]"

      SELECT THE LOCAL OPTION

      The wording on your options is likely different. The key is to select the run local option and to avoid Docker or Podman.

    1. Finally, IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder will request to confirm that all of the details you have provided are accurate.

      • Select the option that allows the build to proceed
      • In this case: "No - proceed with the build [...]"

    1. With every question answered, consider the Auto-approve Permissions you wish to grant Bob. Press the Shield (Permissions) icon next to the mode selector to open these settings.

      For ease of use, and to avoid repetitive clicking, we enabled Auto-approve and toggled all permissions on.

      CONSIDER THE CONTROL YOU HAND OVER

      Weigh the level of control you are comfortable delegating to IBM Bob. If auto-approvals sit uneasily with you, this flow can be completed perfectly well with every auto-approval disabled and purely manual approvals. You will simply need to click "Approve" or "Approve for Task" quite a few more times throughout the lab.

    v. The autonomous build

    With auto-approve enabled, the build now proceeds without interruption — your role across the following steps is to observe. What you are watching is the Demo Builder's methodology executing end-to-end: a structured build plan, parallel generation, and a self-review pass that catches and repairs issues before the work is declared complete.

    1. Success — IBM Bob starts working! At this point the setup is done; the remaining task is to wait while Bob's Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode finishes.

      Creating the actual demo will take around 20 minutes and approximately 15–20 Bobcoins.

    1. IBM Bob has begun building the demo. Within the Agentic Sidebar you can see it has laid out a 5-step build plan: scaffold and environment setup; synthetic data generator; back-end (FastAPI + LangGraph agent); front-end (Carbon React, 5 pages); and Docker/scripts/docs. Bob works through these in parallel wherever no file dependencies exist.

    1. Bob confirms the scaffold is in place and begins generating all files simultaneously. You can see it writing demos/new-demo/.env.example and starting on the back-end in parallel.

    1. Bob is actively writing all back-end files: config.py, requirements.txt, the synthetic data service, and the watsonx service are all being created within demos/new-demo/backend/.

    1. With the back-end complete, Bob moves on to building the full React/Carbon front-end. You can see it writing package.json, vite.config.js, and index.html for the frontend (14 files changed so far.)

    1. Bob continues building the front-end, writing all 5 pages and shared components. You can also see it generating the Dockerfiles, startup scripts, and documentation files (README, PILOT_PLAN.md, ARCHITECTURE.md, DEMO_SCRIPT.md) in parallel. At this stage, 26 files have been updated and changed.

    1. Bob has finished generating all files and is now self-reviewing for issues.

      It has identified a known Carbon icon compatibility problem. Some @carbon/react Sass imports and icon names need to be validated against the Carbon v11 specification before the app can build cleanly.

    1. Bob resolves the Carbon icon issues autonomously, searching through the frontend source files for uncertain icon names and replacing them with confirmed valid @carbon/icons-react v11 alternatives.

    1. Bob continues the icon sweep, applying targeted diffs to ProblemPage.jsx, SolutionPage.jsx, and App.jsx to replace any icon references that may not exist in Carbon v11 with known-safe alternatives.

    1. Bob consolidates all Carbon icon imports to a verified safe set, confirming each icon name against the @carbon/icons-react v11 library.

      This self-correction step is what ensures the app will build without errors.

    1. Bob performs a final sweep:

      • Verifying icon imports across all frontend source files
      • Cleaning up unused imports
      • Running a last structural check on backend/main.py before wrapping up

    1. Bob continues its self-review:

      • Cleaning up unused imports within SolutionPage.jsx
      • Verifying that the ChartMultitype and Diagram references in App.jsx are valid Carbon v11 components
      • Replaces any uncertain names with confirmed safe alternatives such as BarChart and Document
      • Runs a final search across all frontend source files to confirm no remaining icon issues exist

      At this stage, 42 files changed have been updated and modified.

    1. Bob wraps up its quality pass. All icon imports are consolidated to a verified safe set (File, Icons, Safety, and others confirmed valid within @carbon/icons-react v11).

      Bob confirms the backend main.py has no missing module references, and that the project structure is complete and ready to run.

    1. Bob finishes and presents the completed build summary within the Agentic Sidebar.

      Look for the "To Start Now" section in the chat output, as it contains the exact terminal commands needed to run the app locally. The build consumed approximately 15 Bobcoins and modified 42 files.

    vi. Start and test the demo

    1. In the IBM Bob IDE menu bar along the top of your screen, click Terminal to open the Terminal menu. An integrated terminal is needed to run the startup commands Bob provided.

    1. Click New Terminal (or press Ctrl+Shift+ `) to open an integrated terminal panel at the bottom of the IDE. This is where the startup commands will run.

    1. Occasionally, the startup steps you initially receive for your app may not work. Whether it proves a simple syntax issue or a larger problem, getting past it is straightforward: in natural language, tell IBM Bob there is an error, and paste the entire console log.

    1. When an error surfaces, Bob will find a solution quickly. If the provided solution does not work on the first attempt, keep iterating in the same fashion: simply copy and paste the console output into the Agentic Sidebar.

    1. Bob has now provided new commands to start the app. The commands you receive will probably differ from those documented here.

      What is likely to remain consistent is that you will work in two separate Terminal windows, one each for the back-end and the front-end.

    1. In the back-end terminal, run the first back-end command provided by Bob.

      In this example, cd into the demos/new-demo/backend directory. Your path will reflect your own machine's Downloads location.

    1. Run the second back-end command: activate the Python virtual environment by running .\venv\Scripts\Activate.ps1.

      Once active, your Terminal prompt will show (venv) to confirm that the environment is ready.

    1. Run the final back-end command: uvicorn main:app --reload --port 8000.

      You should see Uvicorn report that it is running on http://127.0.0.1:8000, confirming the FastAPI backend is live and watching for changes.

    1. Open a second Terminal tab for the front-end.

      Run the first front-end command to cd into the demos/new-demo/frontend directory.

    1. Run npm install to install all front-end dependencies. You should see output confirming that packages were audited with no vulnerabilities.

    1. Run npm run dev to start the Vite development server. When it finishes, you will see a Local: http://localhost:5173/ (or similar port) link within the terminal output (this is the URL for your demo app.)

    1. Hold Ctrl and click the http://localhost:517x/ link that appeared in the front-end terminal output to open the demo app within your browser.

    1. Upon accessing the site, you may initially see a white screen. Wait 1–2 minutes for the site to load.

      TROUBLESHOOTING

      If the white screen persists, refresh or switch browsers. If the issue still persists, take a screenshot and describe the issue to Bob in the Agentic Sidebar, as practiced previously.

    1. Success — the demo loaded!

      TROUBLESHOOTING

      Notice any issues? Fixing them is simple: take a screenshot and send it to IBM Bob. Here, the sidebar panel is stuck.

      Simply describe the issue in natural language — providing a screenshot where possible — and Bob will fix it.

      Fixed! Solve any issues you may experience with your app (a complete crash, poor aesthetics, and so on) in a similar fashion: describe the problem in natural language and provide screenshots.

    vii. The one-click quickstart script

    1. To avoid the manual startup sequence in the future, simply ask Bob to make a quickstart script, like so:

      Create a quickstart script so I can start my demo in one click, no terminal windows

    1. Bob will generate the script.

      To locate it quickly on your local machine, right-click any of the files on the IBM Bob sidebar and press "Reveal in File Explorer".

    1. Inspect the contents of the /IBM-Pre-Sales-Demo-Builder/ directory and drill down into the /demos/ sub-directory.

    1. Within the /demos directory you should see a newly-created /new-demo subdirectory. Double-click it to expose the contents.

    1. In this example, IBM Bob created a script called START_DEMO.bat for a Windows machine. The file name and type you receive will differ, though the naming will likely be similar. Look for something carrying START or similar.

    1. Run (double-click) the demo script .BAT file.

      TROUBLESHOOTING

      Here, some of the Terminal windows that open appear troublesome, with error logs — but Bob built in fallbacks to address this. If your quickstart script crashes, take a screenshot (or copy and paste the console output) and send it to Bob.

    2. The quickstart script continues. You may experience completely different behaviour.

      Expect multiple Terminal windows to open. Do not close them. Leave them running while you access the demo app.

    1. Another demo script window opens. Depending on how your quickstart script is wired, the app may open automatically or you may need to click it yourself.

      • Look for a line reading http://localhost:517x/..., then hold Ctrl and click it
      • In this specific example, the app started on port 5175, since other ports were already in use
      • On a fresh run-through, your app will typically sit at* http://localhost:5173

    viii. Explore the demo

    1. Back at the localhost URL within a web browser, the demo app appears once again. Click and scroll around, exploring the app to make sure no bugs are lurking.

    1. The current page of the demo app looks correct. Continue clicking through the other pages to verify there are no broken layouts or missing data before proceeding.

    1. Here is the fully-realized AI Agent. You may have a similar web app with multiple tabs (or everything might sit on one page.) You will return here shortly; continue exploring for now.

    1. The architecture diagram looks good. Return to the AI Agent.

    1. Select one of the scenarios. Here, we selected the CNC Spindle Bearing Wear scenario.

    1. The moment of truth: click Analyze Equipment (or a similar blue button) to run the AI analysis demo.

    1. Success! Review the analysis.

      If you experience errors here, simply screenshot them and send them to Bob, as before.

    1. Try another scenario — Hydraulic Press Seal Failure. Here, an option appears to auto-create a (mock) work order. Try it.

    1. Success — work order created!

      Feel free to continue exploring your demo app, refining it as you wish. With a functioning demonstration in hand, the core of this lab is complete.

    ix. Pitching the demo script

    1. Back within the IBM Bob IDE, look inside the /demos folder.

      You will see a Markdown file named something similar to DEMO_SCRIPT.md — click on it.

    1. Right-click and press "Open Preview" within IBM Bob to see a formatted version of the script.

    1. Finally, observe that IBM Bob's Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode created an accompanying preflight checklist and verbal presentation script to sit alongside your demo app. To modify it, simply describe the desired changes to Bob.

      Congratulations — you have now built a demo with IBM Bob!

    x. Key takeaways

    • The Business Partner path is fully self-contained: a packaged download from Seismic, unzipped and opened within the Bob IDE, with the IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode pre-loaded (no Marketplace, tokens, or MCP credentials required)
    • The Shield (Permissions) icon governs auto-approval: full auto-approve keeps the build uninterrupted, while manual approvals preserve step-by-step control at the cost of more clicking
    • A complete build takes roughly 20 minutes and 15–20 Bobcoins, and includes a self-review pass in which Bob validates and repairs its own work (the Carbon v11 icon sweep) before declaring completion
    • The error-iteration pattern (pasting the console log or a screenshot into the Agentic Sidebar, in natural language) is the recommended remedy for startup issues, bugs, and aesthetic refinements alike
    • A Bob-generated quickstart script collapses the two-terminal startup sequence into a single click
    • DEMO_SCRIPT.md turns the build into a presentation: a preflight checklist plus a verbal walkthrough, modifiable on request
