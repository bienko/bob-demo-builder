    BOB DEMO-BUILDER MODE

    DEPLOYING WITH DEMO BUILDER

    i. From concept to demo

    Christopher Bienko (AI Enablement GTM Product Manager) provides an overview of deploying with the Bob Demo Builder hands-on enablement. [3 min]

    Before continuing, a brief recap on where things stand and the work you've carried out so far:

    PRE-REQUISITES

    1. IBM Bob is installed and authenticated (Chapter 1 — the Setup page for your audience)

    2. The IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder is in place within your workspace — installed from the CE Bob Marketplace (IBMers) or unpacked from the Seismic package (Business Partners)

    3. The workspace has been verified and the IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode is visible within the Agentic Sidebar

    Now it's time to put this preparation into practice. In the chapter ahead, you will take a one-sentence use case — an AI agent that watches factory equipment and heads off failures before they occur — and carry it through the Demo Builder's full lifecycle:

    TO-DO'S

    1. Guided intake

    2. Autonomous build

    3. Local launch

    4. One-click quickstart script

    5. Review-and-refine pass

    6. Client-ready demo script

    The flow documented could be described as a "simplified" example and it's been intentionally designed as such to minimize the complexity of your first hands-on experience with this process. It also keeps the Bob tokens consumption to a minimum. For those wanting a more elaborate and resource-intensive example, you will have the opportunity to practice this in the chapters ahead.

    For now, with the simplified example of this chapter, you will build in mock mode. This approach requires zero credentials and works fully offline. There is no watsonx.ai environment to provision and no API key to supply; the workspace you configured in the previous chapter is all that is needed. The purpose is to experience the complete arc of the Demo Builder methodology end-to-end, and to establish repeatable patterns that you can apply (with fuller briefs and real environments) to potential client opportunities at a later time.

    ii. Why this matters

    The division of labor in this chapter is the discipline this course has been reinforcing from the beginning: Bob does the building; the human-in-the-loop (you) makes the final decisions and calls the shots. The intake questions that the Demo Builder poses at the start are critical decision points that you must answer:

    • The build approach
    • The environment
    • The deployment target
    • The industry scenario

    Once those decisions have been recorded and sanctioned, Bob executes those instructions autonomously. It generates the code and then verifies and tests it. As you will witness first-hand, it also catches and repairs its own bugs along the way. Throughout this process you maintain your role as the principal arbiter: approving additional actions and validating the results, then advancing (or re-orienting) the workflow.

    There is a second lesson embedded here as well. Despite "mock mode" having no live models or real environments backing it, the finalized demonstration is a complete and navigable Carbon-styled application. It ships with preset AI scenarios and a results view. It includes an architecture view and a generated walkthrough script. For engagements where you are short on time (or short on Bob tokens before the month rolls over), these types of lightweight demonstrations can be immensely valuable in a pinch.

    Finally, there is a practical dimension worth internalizing. The full build consumes roughly 20 minutes of time and approximately 15–20 Bobcoins. For a Business Partner especially, that means a credible IBM demonstration no longer requires provisioned environments or weeks of scarce technical talent as its price of admission.

    iii. Briefing the demo builder

    A WORD ON WORKING WITH GENERATIVE AI

    IBM Bob is powered by generative AI and LLMs, and a defining trait of these systems is that they are non-deterministic — unlike the deterministic tools most developers are accustomed to. In practice, that means the same prompt can produce different code from one run to the next. This is both a strength and a quirk of the technology, and it is something to work with rather than against.

    Precision is what tips the odds in your favor. The more clearly you describe what you want, the more closely Bob's output will mirror your intent; conversely, the smallest change in wording can meaningfully change the generated code. Throughout this lab, your results may differ from the solutions shown — and that is expected, not a defect. Whenever an output diverges, you have three good options: refine the prompt and try again, edit the generated code by hand, or simply proceed if the variation still compiles and runs. Human review remains essential at every step, and never more so than while you are still learning the tool.

    The first stage of any Demo Builder engagement is switching into the correct mode and handing Bob a one-sentence use case. From there, Bob will run pre-flight checks and walk you through a short guided intake, where each of Bob's questions is a decision point you must answer.

    1. Within the Agentic Sidebar, click the Modes selector (which is likely displaying Agent mode at this time) to expose more options:

    1. From the drop-down list, switch to the IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode.

    1. With IBM Bob now in IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode, paste the following prompt into the Agentic Sidebar and press Enter to execute.

      I need a demo of an AI agent that monitors IoT sensor data from factory equipment, predicts failures before they happen, and automatically creates maintenance work orders with recommended parts and technicians.

    1. IBM Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode will first run pre-flight checks to ensure everything is prepared and then begins the intake process.

      • Approve the MCP tool prompt
      • Click the Approve for task toggle to reduce the amount of manual approvals required later in the flow

      In the Business Partner run, no MCP tool prompt appeared at this stage and Bob moved directly into the intake questions. If that is what you see, simply carry on.

    SEQUENCING THE INTAKE

    In the steps ahead, you will answer a short series of intake questions that shape the build. Each decision determines a different facet of your finished demo, from the technology stack and environment mode through to the deployment target and industry scenario. The exact set (and order) of questions varies from one run to the next: the two documented runs between them produced the seven decision points described below. Treat any question your run skips as a decision Bob has made for you — and review it at the final confirmation.

    Intake 1: Build Approach

    Bob asks how the AI agent should be built. This selection determines whether the demo uses watsonx.ai (a LangGraph ReAct agent running in a FastAPI backend) or one of the alternative build approaches, such as watsonx Orchestrate. For this walkthrough, choose the watsonx.ai option.

    1. Once the pre-flight checks are completed, the Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode will present a short sequence of questions in order to tailor the demo to your requirements.

      First, it asks "How should the AI agent be built? [...]". Select the top option:

      watsonx.ai (RECOMMENDED) — A LangGraph ReAct agent running in a FastAPI backend.

      YOUR OPTIONS WILL LIKELY DIFFER

      The exact options presented will very likely be different in your run — the key is simply to select the option carrying watsonx.ai. In the Business Partner run, for example, the same choice was labelled as follows:

      "Use suggestion: watsonx.ai App demo (RECOMMENDED) — LangGraph ReAct agent in FastAPI, Carbon dashboard with real-time sensor charts, anomaly det..."

      The watsonx Orchestrate demo setup is more advanced; additional details can be found here: https://github.ibm.com/ClientEngineering/bob/blob/main/Pre-Sales-Demo-Builder/Sample-Reciepies/07-Predictive-Maintenance-Agent.md

    Intake 2: Target Audience

    Bob may ask who the demonstration will be presented to. This selection tailors the framing, level of detail, and pitch length. For this walkthrough, the specific choice does not impact the build.

    1. Next, you may be asked what audience the demonstration should be presented to. This question surfaced in the Business Partner run and was skipped in the IBMer run.

      The selection does not particularly matter for this exercise — here, we chose: "C-suite / VP of Operations / Plant Director — 15-minute executive overview [...]"

    Intake 3: Environment Mode

    Bob asks whether you have a live watsonx.ai environment provisioned. In real engagements, live API keys can be provided to power genuine model responses. For this walkthrough, you will use mock mode, which produces impressive results with zero credentials and works fully offline.

    1. Next, Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode asks whether you already have a watsonx.ai environment provisioned.

      THIS SELECTION IS CRUCIAL

      Select: "No — build in mock mode only (zero credentials needed, works fully offline)" — or whichever option in your run carries mock mode only.

      In a future section, you will explore how this mode operates when an environment actually is provisioned. For now, mock mode presents the complete overview of the capabilities — with none of the credential overhead. As you will see, realistic and impressive results are achievable even upon purely synthetic data.

    Intake 4: Deployment Target

    Bob asks how the app should be deployed. Selecting the run locally option ensures the demo can be launched from your machine without Docker or Podman — the simplest path for a client meeting.

    1. Next, you are asked "How do you want to run and deploy this demo".

      Select the "Run locally" option that fits your specific operating system — typically one of the top two responses. For example:

      Run locally first — I'm on Intel/AMD (amd64) Mac, Linux, or Windows

      SELECT THE LOCAL OPTION

      The wording on your options is likely different. In the Business Partner run it read "Use suggestion: Run locally first (RECOMMENDED default) [...]". The key is to select the run local option and to avoid Docker or Podman.

    Intake 5: Industry Scenario

    Bob may ask which specific factory equipment scenario should be showcased. This selection defines the demo's narrative — the equipment types, the sensor data, and the failure modes that will drive the walkthrough. For this walkthrough, choose the industrial CNC/conveyor/HVAC scenario.

    1. Next, it may ask "What specific factory equipment scenario should be showcased?" This question surfaced in the IBMer run and was skipped in the Business Partner run.

      Click the top option:

      Industrial CNC machines + conveyor belts + HVAC systems \[...\]

      Here you have more flexibility. If a listed industry or scenario aligns better with your own experience or interests, you are welcome to select it. However, in practice your results may drift further from the captures shown in this guide should you opt to do so.

    Intake 6: Final Confirmation

    Bob asks for a final confirmation that the recorded intake selections are correct. Approving here commits the plan and hands execution over to Bob for the autonomous build.

    1. Finally, Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode requests a final confirmation that everything is in order.

      Select the following option to conclude the fact-gathering phase and move ahead with the build instructions:

      No — we have everything, start building now!

      In the Business Partner run, the equivalent option read "No - proceed with the build [...]". Whatever the wording, select the option that allows the build to proceed.

    Intake 7: Auto-Approve Permissions

    Before the build begins, decide how much autonomy Bob receives. Enabling Auto-approve eliminates repetitive clicking during the 20-minute build, at the cost of stepping back from each individual action.

    1. With every question answered, consider the Auto-approve Permissions you wish to grant Bob. Press the Shield (Permissions) icon next to the mode selector to open these settings.

      For ease of use, and to avoid repetitive clicking, the Business Partner run enabled Auto-approve and toggled all permissions on. The IBMer run left auto-approve off and relied on Approve for task at each checkpoint instead; the steps ahead document that run.

      CONSIDER THE CONTROL YOU HAND OVER

      Weigh the level of control you are comfortable delegating to IBM Bob. If auto-approvals sit uneasily with you, this flow can be completed perfectly well with every auto-approval disabled and purely manual approvals. You will simply need to click "Approve" or "Approve for Task" quite a few more times throughout the lab.

    iv. The autonomous build

    With the intake complete, the balance of the work shifts to Bob. Your role across the following steps encompasses oversight and steering: review what Bob proposes and either approve the workflow or course-correct as necessary.

    Bob will execute the build across the following (or similar) sub-tasks:

    TASK OBJECTIVES
    Sub-Task 1 Scaffold project subdirectories
    Sub-Task 2 Build the back-end
    Sub-Task 3 Run back-end verification
    Sub-Task 4 Run back-end tests
    Sub-Task 5 Build the front-end
    Sub-Task 6 Run final compliance tests

    In the Business Partner run, Bob organized the same work as a 5-step build plan: scaffold and environment setup; a synthetic data generator; the back-end (FastAPI + LangGraph agent); the front-end (Carbon React, 5 pages); and Docker/scripts/docs. It worked through these in parallel wherever no file dependencies existed, then closed with a self-review pass. The destination is the same either way.

    Sub-Task 1: Initial Kickoff and Scaffolding

    Bob confirms the intake decisions and begins scaffolding the project. This includes creating the subdirectories that will store the front-end and back-end, along with the data and documentation for the demo.

    1. Bob begins working. As usual, approve tasks when prompted (if you agree with Bob's recommended course of action.)

    Sub-Task 2: Build the Back-End

    Bob creates the subdirectories and begins generating the back-end files: the configuration and the FastAPI service first, then the LangGraph agent and the synthetic data layer.

    1. Bob creates subdirectories that will store the project.

    Sub-Task 3: Run Back-End Verification

    With the back-end files in place, Bob runs a verification pass to confirm the code is structurally sound before executing any tests.

    1. At last, the back-end build has completed and IBM Bob's Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode is now running the build verification.

    Sub-Task 4: Run Back-End Tests

    Bob executes the automated back-end tests. This step validates that the FastAPI service and the LangGraph agent behave as expected before the front-end build begins.

    1. Pre-Sales Demo Builder mode now runs the back-end tests.

      SELF-REVIEW IN ACTION: THE CARBON ICON SWEEP (BUSINESS PARTNER RUN)

      The self-review pass takes a different shape on every run. In the Business Partner run, Bob finished generating all files and then identified a known Carbon icon compatibility problem: some @carbon/react Sass imports and icon names needed to be validated against the Carbon v11 specification before the app could build cleanly. With auto-approve enabled, the entire repair unfolded without a single click.

      Bob resolved the Carbon icon issues autonomously, searching through the frontend source files for uncertain icon names and replacing them with confirmed valid @carbon/icons-react v11 alternatives.

      The icon sweep continued with targeted diffs to ProblemPage.jsx and SolutionPage.jsx as well as App.jsx. These replaced any icon references that may not exist in Carbon v11 with known-safe alternatives.

      Bob then consolidated all Carbon icon imports to a verified safe set, confirming each icon name against the @carbon/icons-react v11 library. This self-correction step is what ensures the app will build without errors.

      A final sweep followed: verifying icon imports across all frontend source files; cleaning up unused imports; and running a last structural check on backend/main.py before wrapping up.

      Bob continued its self-review by cleaning up unused imports within SolutionPage.jsx and verifying that the ChartMultitype and Diagram references in App.jsx were valid Carbon v11 components. Uncertain names were replaced with confirmed safe alternatives such as BarChart and Document. At this stage, 42 files had been updated and modified.

      Bob wrapped up its quality pass with all icon imports consolidated to a verified safe set (File, Icons and Safety among others), each confirmed valid within @carbon/icons-react v11. It also confirmed that the backend main.py had no missing module references.

    v. Launch the demo

    With the build complete and verified, the next stage is to launch the demo locally. This is a two-terminal sequence: one for the FastAPI back-end, and one for the Vite front-end. Bob provides the exact commands in its final completion message. If something breaks along the way, the recommended remedy is to paste the console log directly into the Agentic Sidebar and let Bob diagnose the issue.

    1. At this stage, the demo build has been completed and verified.

      Read through the final completion message and locate the Terminal commands (documented within the console read-out) to start the application.

      In the Business Partner run, the same information was presented under a "To Start Now" section of the build summary. That build consumed approximately 15 Bobcoins and modified 42 files.

      TROUBLESHOOTING: THE STARTUP COMMANDS DO NOT WORK

      Occasionally, the startup steps you initially receive for your app may not work. Whether it proves a simple syntax issue or a larger problem, getting past it is simple enough. Tell IBM Bob in natural language that there is an error and paste the entire console log.

      When an error surfaces, Bob will find a solution quickly. If the provided solution does not work on the first attempt, keep iterating in the same fashion: simply copy and paste the console output into the Agentic Sidebar.

      Bob will then provide new commands to start the app. The commands you receive will probably differ from those documented here. What is likely to remain consistent is that you will work in two separate Terminal windows, one each for the back-end and the front-end.

    1. Launch a new Terminal within the Bob IDE by clicking Terminal > New Terminal from the top-line menu bar.

    1. Copy the Terminal 1 - Backend command line string (from Step 18) to your clipboard, paste it into the new Terminal console, and hit Enter to execute.

      Execute these instructions line by line, one at a time.

      Continue executing each instruction from Terminal 1 - Backend until the service has successfully started.

      WHAT THE BACK-END COMMANDS TYPICALLY LOOK LIKE (BUSINESS PARTNER RUN)

      The first back-end command changes into the back-end directory. In this example, that meant a cd into demos/new-demo/backend. Your path will reflect your own machine's Downloads location.

      The second back-end command activates the Python virtual environment: .\venv\Scripts\Activate.ps1 on Windows. Once active, your Terminal prompt will show (venv) to confirm that the environment is ready.

      The final back-end command starts the service: uvicorn main:app --reload --port 8000. You should see Uvicorn report that it is running on http://127.0.0.1:8000, confirming the FastAPI backend is live and watching for changes.

    1. With the back-end service successfully started, you will need to open a second Terminal console to run the front-end service.

      As before, click Terminal > New Terminal from the top-line menu of the Bob IDE. (Alternatively, click the + button to open a new Terminal window.)

    1. Copy the Terminal 2 - Frontend command line string (from Step 18) to your clipboard, paste it into the new Terminal console, and hit Enter to execute.

      Execute these instructions line by line, one at a time.

      Continue executing each instruction from Terminal 2 - Frontend until the service has successfully started.

      WHAT THE FRONT-END COMMANDS TYPICALLY LOOK LIKE (BUSINESS PARTNER RUN)

      The first front-end command changes into the front-end directory: a cd into demos/new-demo/frontend.

      Running npm install then installs all front-end dependencies. You should see output confirming that packages were audited with no vulnerabilities.

    1. To finish starting up the app, the final instruction to be executed within the Terminal console should resemble something like the following:

      npm run dev

      When it finishes, you will see a Local: http://localhost:5173/ (or similar port) link within the terminal output. This is the URL for your demo app.

    1. If successful, the Terminal console should report back that the app has started.

      Hold Ctrl and then left-click the URL to open the address embedded within the Terminal console to view the application with your default web browser.

      A WHITE SCREEN ON FIRST LOAD

      Upon accessing the site, you may initially see a white screen. Wait 1–2 minutes for the site to load. If the white screen persists, refresh or switch browsers. If the issue still persists, take a screenshot and describe the issue to Bob in the Agentic Sidebar.

      TROUBLESHOOTING: A STUCK SIDEBAR PANEL

      Notice any issues once the demo loads? Fixing them is simple: take a screenshot and send it to IBM Bob. In the Business Partner run, the sidebar panel was stuck.

      Simply describe the issue in natural language — providing a screenshot where possible — and Bob will fix it.

      Solve any issues you may experience with your app (a complete crash, poor aesthetics, and so on) in a similar fashion: describe the problem in natural language and provide screenshots.

    vi. The one-click quickstart script

    The two-terminal startup sequence works, but it is not what you want to be running in front of a client. The next stage condenses the entire launch into a single double-click by asking Bob to generate a quickstart script. Remember that IBM Bob is remarkably flexible: if something is unclear to you at any point in the process, simply explain it to Bob in natural language.

    1. To avoid the manual startup sequence in the future, simply ask Bob to make a quickstart script, like so:

      Create a quickstart script so I can start my demo in one click, no terminal windows

      The wording is yours to choose. The IBMer run asked for the same thing as follows, with the same result:

      Make a quickstart script that allows me to start the app in 1 click, no manual Terminal windows.

    1. Bob will generate the script.

      To locate it quickly on your local machine, right-click any of the files on the IBM Bob sidebar and press "Reveal in File Explorer".

    1. Inspect the contents of the /IBM-Pre-Sales-Demo-Builder/ directory (or, for IBMers, your Bob Demo Builder workspace) and drill down into the /demos/ sub-directory.

    1. Within the /demos directory you should see a newly-created /new-demo subdirectory. Double-click it to expose the contents.

    1. In this example, IBM Bob created a script called START_DEMO.bat for a Windows machine. The file name and type you receive will differ, though the naming will likely be similar. Look for something carrying START or similar.

    1. Run (double-click) the demo script .BAT file.

      TROUBLESHOOTING

      Here, some of the Terminal windows that open appear troublesome, with error logs — but Bob built in fallbacks to address this. If your quickstart script crashes, take a screenshot (or copy and paste the console output) and send it to Bob.

    1. The quickstart script continues. You may experience completely different behaviour.

      Expect multiple Terminal windows to open. Do not close them. Leave them running while you access the demo app.

    1. Another demo script window opens. Depending on how your quickstart script is wired, the app may open automatically or you may need to click it yourself.

      • Look for a line reading http://localhost:517x/..., then hold Ctrl and click it
      • In this specific example, the app started on port 5175, since other ports were already in use
      • On a fresh run-through, your app will typically sit at http://localhost:5173

    ix. Key takeaways

    • The simplified flow carries a one-sentence use case through the Demo Builder's complete lifecycle: brief → intake → build → launch → refine → present. The same procedure applies whether the Demo Builder arrived via the CE Bob Marketplace or the Seismic package.
    • Large Demo Builder tasks are best performed with Approve for task (or Auto-approve) enabled. This allows Bob to advance through the back-end build and its verification and tests, and then through the front-end build and compliance pass, without repeated interruption.
    • The guided intake questionnaire covers the essential decision points that you author, and Bob actions: the build approach and audience; the environment and deployment target; the scenario; and the final confirmation. The exact set of questions varies between runs.
    • Mock mode produces a complete, navigable, client-credible demonstration with zero credentials and a fully offline environment — ideal for time-constrained engagements or when conserving Bob tokens.
    • A complete build takes roughly 20 minutes and consumes approximately 15–20 Bobcoins.
    • Bob's self-review methodology is on full display during the final compliance pass — catching its own bugs (such as the Carbon v11 icon sweep), investigating them autonomously, and applying repairs before declaring the build complete.
    • The screenshot + natural-language prompt pattern — pasting console logs or screenshots (or simply a description) into the Agentic Sidebar — is the recommended remedy for startup issues and bugs, and for aesthetic refinements alike. If the Agentic Sidebar freezes or times out, the continue prompt reliably resumes your work where it left off.
    • A Bob-generated quickstart script collapses the two-terminal startup sequence into a single click, eliminating manual setup at client meetings.
    • The generated DEMO_SCRIPT.md transforms the build into a full presentation package: a preflight checklist plus a verbal walkthrough, modifiable on request.
    • Human review remains essential throughout the process. Carefully reviewing intake selections, the running app, and the generated pitch script ensures a successful and trustworthy demonstration outcome.

    In the following chapter, you will learn to author the demo brief yourself according to best practices — the six things every prompt must answer — to produce demos that are expertly tailored to a specific client and industry, and to the audience in the room.
